I N T R O D U C T I O N
The new reality of safety in
Nigeria
We have to begin honestly, because pretending all is well is
itself a kind of danger. The Nigeria many of us grew up in —
where you knew everyone on your street, trusted the road to
the village, and never thought twice about the school gate —
has changed. None of us caused that change, and no amount
of worrying will undo it. But seeing it clearly, with calm eyes,
is the very first thing that keeps a family safe.
Consider what a single recent year looked like. The National
Bureau of Statistics' Crime Experience and Security
Perception Survey — the most comprehensive study of its
kind, drawn from households across all 36 states and the FCT
and covering May 2023 to April 2024 — estimated about 2.2
million kidnapping incidents in that year alone, with
families paying roughly ₦2.23 trillion in ransom. Of the
households touched by kidnapping, around 65% paid, at an
average of about ₦2.67 million each. The same survey
counted close to 52 million crime incidents in all. These are
not abstractions or foreign headlines. They are our
neighbours, our colleagues, the people we sit beside in
church and at the mosque.
And the pressure has not eased. Independent trackers at
SBM Intelligence recorded at least 4,722 people abducted in
997 incidents in the year to June 2025 — concentrated in the
4

===== PAGE 5 =====
North-West, but with no part of the country truly
untouched. Into 2026, civic groups warned that more than
1,000 Nigerians had been abducted in the first weeks of the
year, and both local and international advisories kept listing
kidnapping, armed robbery, carjacking, and banditry among
the risks of ordinary life.
But — and this matters every bit as much — the picture is not
all darkness, and it is very far from hopeless. In that same
stretch of time, Nigerian banks reported that digital-
payment fraud fell by about 51%, from ₦52.26 billion in 2024
to ₦25.85 billion in 2025. That drop did not happen by luck.
It happened because people and institutions got smarter,
more careful, and harder to fool. And that is the entire
premise of this playbook: awareness works. Preparation
works. The ordinary family is anything but helpless.
This is not a book about fear
Let us be clear from the start about what this guide is — and
what it is not.
It is not here to frighten you. Fear is loud and, in the end,
useless. It freezes you at the exact moment you most need to
think clearly, and when it is left to simmer it quietly shrinks
your whole life, until you are afraid of your own road, your
own gate, your own phone. That is no way for anyone to live,
and it is not what safety means.
This guide is about the calm opposite of fear: awareness,
layers, and a plan you have actually practised. It takes the

same systems that security professionals, soldiers, and
protection experts rely on — systems that can sound
intimidating or far removed from everyday life — and
translates them into plain, practical habits any of us can use.
It does not matter whether you live in a gated estate or a
face-me-I-face-you compound, whether you move around in
your own car or board a danfo every morning. The principles
are the same, and they are all within your reach.
🧭
 The single idea behind this whole book: in a real crisis,
none of us rises to the occasion — we fall back to what
we have already practised. Everything in these pages
exists to give you and your family something calm,
simple, and rehearsed to fall back on.
The one thing every criminal needs
Here is the insight that ties the entire playbook together.
Whether it is an armed robber, a "one chance" gang, a kidnap
crew, a burglar, or a faceless online fraudster, they are all
after the same three things — and they cannot succeed
without them: surprise, isolation, and control.
Surprise — catching you unaware, before you have any
chance to react.
Isolation — getting you alone and cut off from help,
witnesses, or an exit.
Control — once they have your attention, your gate, your
money, or your panic, dictating everything that happens
next.

Take away even one of these and most attacks simply fall
apart. Awareness destroys surprise. Layers and good daily
habits deny isolation. A calm, practised plan strips away
their control. You do not need to be a fighter, wealthy, or
armed to be safe. You only need to be a harder, less
predictable target than the situation around you — and that,
every one of us can become.
Who this playbook is for
This is written for the ordinary Nigerian family that wants to
live fully and freely — to travel, do business, raise children,
build a home — without handing easy opportunities to the
few who would prey on them. It is for the parent planning
the school run, the driver on the long interstate road, the
young professional taking a ride home after dark, the trader
protecting her day's earnings, the family putting up their first
gate. Our circumstances differ; the calm, layered approach
belongs to all of us equally.
What's inside, and how it fits together
The playbook moves from the everyday to the rare, and from
preventing trouble to responding well if it ever finds you.
Each module stands on its own, but read together they form
one connected system:
Module 1 — Situational awareness: seeing trouble early,
before it ever reaches you. The foundation everything else
is built on.

How to use this playbook
Read it once, calmly, all the way through — ideally long
before you ever need it. Knowing the whole picture helps
each piece fall into place.
Start with Modules 1 and 2. Awareness and daily
movement protect you every single day, wherever you live.
Do the checklist at the end of each module before moving
on. Reading changes what you know; the checklists change
what you do.
Fill in the worksheets. They quietly turn this from a book
about safety into your family's own safety plan.
Build your Family Emergency Plan (Module 8) within
your first week — even a rough version. You can refine it
later.
Keep the Quick-Reference Cards on your phone, your
fridge, and in your car, so they are within reach in the few
seconds that truly count.
Bring your family in. A plan only one person knows is half
a plan. Share it — gently and calmly, never as a fear lecture
— with your spouse, your children, and the people who
help keep your home.
💚
 One last word before we begin. Choosing to read this is
not paranoia — it is care made practical. It is love,
turned into something useful. You cannot control
everything that happens in this country, but you can
quietly make your family a great deal safer than it was
yesterday. That work starts on the very next page.

C H A P T E R  1
Situational Awareness
Seeing trouble before it reaches you
If you learn nothing else from this playbook, learn this
module. Almost every other chapter is about reacting well.
This one is about never needing to react at all — because
awareness is what lets you step out of danger before it closes
around you.
Here is the single most important truth about serious crime
in Nigeria: most attacks are not random. Armed robbers,
"one chance" gangs, and kidnap crews are running a
business, and like any business they reduce their risk before
they act. They choose targets who look unaware, predictable,
and isolated. They watch. They test. Then they move. Every
step they take before the attack is also a chance for you to
notice — and noticing is free.
Think of awareness not as fear or paranoia, but as a quiet
background habit, like checking your mirrors when you
drive. You are not expecting a crash. You are simply keeping
enough information flowing that, if something goes wrong,
you already have somewhere to go.

===== PAGE 11 =====
🧭
 The core principle: Criminals need three things to
succeed — surprise, isolation, and control. Awareness
destroys surprise. The rest of this playbook helps you
deny them isolation and control. Take away surprise
and you have already won most of the fight.
A familiar pattern (and how awareness breaks it)
Picture a common Lagos morning. A woman leaves her estate
at exactly 7:10 every weekday, turns right, and joins the same
go-slow at the same junction, phone pressed to her ear,
window half down. For three days, a man on an okada has
been parked near her gate when she comes out. She half-
noticed him on day one, forgot by day two, and on day three
didn't look up at all.
Nothing in that story is dramatic — and that is exactly the
point. The danger was never hidden. It was routine,
predictable, and unobserved. Now rewind it: on day two she
clocks the same okada, feels a small unease, varies her time
and route on day three, mentions it to her gateman, and the
watcher — who needs an easy, predictable target — quietly
moves on to someone else. That is situational awareness
working the way it is supposed to: boring, invisible, and life-
saving.

first popularised by firearms instructor Jeff Cooper, often

How criminals choose a target: the "marking"
phase
Before a planned kidnapping or home invasion, there is
almost always a period of watching — Nigerians call it being 
"marked." The crew learns your routine: when you leave,
when you return, who lives with you, how many guards,
which gate, whether you travel alone. The more predictable
you are, the easier you are to plan around.
This surveillance is also the criminal's most exposed moment
— and your best early-warning window. Watch for:
The same face, car, or okada appearing near your gate,
office, or your children's school run more than once,
especially at the times you come and go.
People loitering near your gate with no clear reason, or
"workmen, " "meter readers, " or "officials" no one actually
called.
Strangers asking questions — of your gateman,
neighbours, shop owners, or children — about your
movements, your schedule, who owns which car, or when
"oga" travels.
A parked car with people just sitting inside near your
home or a regular stop, particularly at odd hours.
Repeated silent or wrong-number calls, or a "delivery"
you never ordered — sometimes used to confirm you are
home or to get your gate open.

The other danger zone: transitions
Most attacks happen not in open movement but at transition
points — the few seconds when you are slowing down,
stopping, or distracted. The highest-risk moments in an
ordinary day are:
Arriving home and waiting for the gate to open — you are
stationary, boxed in, and predictable. This is the classic
kidnap and "follow-home" robbery moment.
Leaving home in the morning at the same time each day.
Getting in or out of your car at a shop, ATM, church, or
school.
Stuck in go-slow with windows down and attention on
your phone.
For each of these, the fix is the same: shift to Gear 2 before
you arrive, keep space and an exit, and never sit unaware.
(Module 3 covers the gate-arrival drill in detail; Module 5
covers the car.)
Trust the feeling: your instinct is data
That small, wordless sense that something is off is not
superstition — it is your brain noticing a pattern faster than
it can explain it. Security experts call it the body's early-
warning system, and the single biggest mistake good people
make is overriding it to avoid seeming rude.
You do not owe a stranger your politeness at the cost of your
safety. If a situation feels wrong — a man who is suddenly too
close, a "helper" you didn't ask for, a lift that feels off — you

are allowed to leave, lock the door, refuse, or walk into a
shop, with no explanation and no apology. The worst case if
you're wrong is mild awkwardness. The worst case if you

The 3-turn follow test
If you suspect a vehicle or okada is following you on foot or
while driving, confirm it calmly before reacting:
Make three deliberate, ordinary turns — for example,
three right turns that bring you back toward where you
started, or turns onto streets you would have no normal
reason to take.
If the same vehicle stays with you through all three,
treat it as a follow. Do not speed, panic, or confront.
Drive to safety, not home. Head for the nearest police or
military checkpoint, fuel station, bank, or busy, well-lit
public place. Call someone and say where you are. Never
lead a follower to your gate — that hands them exactly the
isolation they want.
📞
 Say this on the phone (out loud, even to voicemail): "I
think I'm being followed. I'm on [road], heading to [the
filling station / police post] at [landmark]. The car is a
[colour, type, plate if visible]. Stay on with me." Saying it
aloud calms you, creates a record, and can deter a
watcher who realises you've clocked them.

Awareness in everyday Nigerian settings
On foot / at markets and bus stops. Keep your phone in
your pocket, not your face. Carry your bag on the side away
from the road to deter okada snatch-and-grab. Pick the
busier, better-lit path even if it's slightly longer. At a bus
stop, stand back from the kerb and near other people.
Okada, keke, and "drop" cabs. Before you enter, glance at
who else is inside and whether it feels normal. A half-empty
"drop" with two or three "passengers" who insist you squeeze
in is the classic "one chance" setup — wave it on and take the
next one. (Full ride-hailing and "one chance" guidance is in
Module 2.)
Banks and ATMs. Scan before you approach; don't use a
machine in a lonely or dark spot; never count cash in the
open. Anyone who follows you out of a bank deserves your
full attention.
Online — the invisible surveillance. Today much "marking"
happens on a phone screen. Posting your new car, your travel
dates, your children's school or uniform, or live "I'm at…"
locations does a criminal's homework for them. Awareness
now includes your digital footprint. (Module 7 goes deep on
this.)

Teach your whole household to see
Awareness only protects a family if the whole family practises
it. Make it normal dinner-table talk, not a fear lecture.
Children: teach them, simply, to notice if "the same person
keeps showing up, " to never go with anyone without the
family code word (Module 4), and to run toward people
and noise — into a shop, a crowd — if they feel scared.
Spouse and adult relatives: agree that anyone can call a
stop with no debate. If one person feels off, the family acts
first and discusses later.
Gateman, driver, and domestic staff: they are your front
line and a common target for questioning. Brief them
never to share your schedule, travel plans, or movements
with anyone, and to report strangers asking questions or
loitering. (Vetting them properly is covered in Module 3.)
A 30-second daily drill
Awareness is a muscle. Train it with this simple game until
it's reflex:
The exit habit: each time you enter a place — a restaurant,
bank, church, shop — find the exits within 30 seconds.
You'll never need most of them. The one time you do,
you'll already know.
The recall game: occasionally, look away and ask yourself,
"What colour was the car that just passed? Who is
standing behind me?" If you can't answer, you've slipped
into Gear 1. Climb back to Gear 2.

Common myths that get people hurt
"It won't happen to me / on my street." Predictability, not
neighbourhood, is what marks a target. Awareness is
cheap insurance regardless of where you live.
"Being aware means being paranoid and tense all day." The
opposite — true awareness is calm and quiet. Panic is what
happens when you weren't aware and got surprised.
"If I'm polite and cooperative, nothing bad will happen."
Politeness toward a stranger who triggers your instinct is
exactly the reflex criminals rely on. Be courteous to people
you trust; be safe around everyone else.
"My gateman/guard handles security." Guards help, but
they can be deceived, distracted, or questioned for
information. Your own awareness is the layer no one can
switch off.

C H A P T E R  2
Daily Movement Safety
Commuting, traffic, ATMs & ride-hailing
Module 1 taught you to see trouble. This module is about the
place you are most likely to meet it: on the move. For the
average Nigerian family, the daily commute, the school run,
the quick dash to an ATM, and the long go-slow home are
where exposure is highest — not because movement is rare,
but because it is routine, predictable, and distracted.
The good news is that the same small habits protect you
across every kind of trip, whether you drive your own car,
take Bolt, or squeeze into a danfo. This module gives you
those habits, broken down by how you actually travel.
🧭
 The movement rule of three: before any trip, ask — 
Who knows where I'm going? What's my exit if this goes
wrong? Am I in Gear 2? If you can answer all three,
you've already removed most of the risk.
Know the threats you're actually facing
"One chance." We all know the name — it's an organised
robbery run from a moving vehicle, usually a car or keke. A
car or keke that already contains the gang hails you as an
ordinary passenger; once you're in and moving, the doors
effectively lock, and you are robbed, beaten, or driven off. It
peaks at rush hour in Lagos and Abuja, when everyone is
21

===== PAGE 22 =====
tired, rushing, and grateful for any ride. Abuja hotspots
repeatedly named in reporting include Banex Junction, CBN
Junction, Airport Road, Central Area, Garki, Nicon Junction,
Kubwa Expressway, Mabushi, Zuba, and the Gwagwalada–
Lokoja road. A November 2025 diplomatic security advisory
specifically flagged a rise in "one chance" vehicle robberies
and kidnappings across Abuja and Lagos, including a
commercial-taxi driver arrested for repeated robberies and
assaults on passengers, especially women.
Express kidnapping. Increasingly, "one chance" escalates
into what security professionals call express kidnapping:
rather than a long ransom negotiation, the victim is held for a
few hours and driven between ATMs, forced to withdraw the
daily maximum and to transfer money or hand over banking
credentials. It usually ends "when the money runs out" —
when daily withdrawal limits are hit or the bank blocks the
account. Crucially, experts note these incidents are usually
non-violent unless the victim resists — which shapes how
you respond (see below).
A familiar scenario
It's 7:40 pm at a busy Lagos bus stop. A cab slows, already
holding two quiet "passengers, " and the driver waves you in
with a friendly "enter, last seat!" You're tired, it's getting dark,
and the next bus is nowhere. The pull to just get in is
enormous — and that pull is exactly what the gang is selling.
The person who goes home safe is the one who notices the
small wrong notes — too eager a driver, passengers who
22

===== PAGE 23 =====
won't meet your eye, a car that's oddly empty for rush hour
— and simply says "no, I dey wait for person" and steps back.
There is no prize for being the polite passenger. Wave it on
and take the next one.
Ride-hailing (Bolt, Uber, inDrive) — your safest
option, used correctly
App-based rides are generally safer than street cabs because
they create a record — but only if you use the safety features.
In 2025, Bolt led Nigerian e-hailing on safety with mandatory
passenger verification and a driver-scoring system; Uber
and inDrive offer their own in-app safety tools. Use them
every time:
Match before you enter: confirm the plate number, car
model/colour, and driver's photo against the app. If any
one doesn't match, do not get in — cancel and rebook.
Share your live trip with a trusted contact by default, on
every ride. Let the driver see you do it.
Stay on-app, always. Never accept "let's go offline, " "cancel
and pay cash, " or a side deal. Off-app trips are invisible —
no GPS trail, no record of the driver, no support if
something goes wrong. This is the single most important
ride-hailing rule.
Sit in the back, behind the front passenger seat — it gives
you the widest view and two exit doors.
Watch the route on the map. If the driver makes
unexplained detours or stops to "pick a friend, " speak up,
call someone, and end the trip at the next safe, busy place.

Trust the cancel button. A poor rating, a vehicle that
doesn't match, or a driver who feels off is reason enough
to cancel before entering. You lose nothing.
Danfo, keke, okada & street cabs
Not everyone can afford an app ride for every trip. If you use
public or informal transport:
Avoid near-empty "drop" cabs that already hold two or
three passengers pushing you to join — the classic "one
chance" setup.
Board at recognised parks and stops, not from a vehicle
that pulls up uninvited and hurries you in.
Glance inside first. If the mood feels off, the passengers
won't look at you, or the door handles/windows look
tampered with, wait for the next one.
Keep your bag closed and on the far side from the
window or road to deter snatch-and-grab by passing
okada.
Sit near the door where it's safe to exit, and stay in Gear 2
— phone away, eyes up.
ATM safety (and the express-kidnap link)
ATMs are a magnet for both ordinary robbery and express
kidnapping, because they turn a victim into instant cash.

Official travel advisories are blunt: use ATMs in public, well-
lit places during daylight hours only.
Choose machines inside a bank branch, mall, or busy
filling station over a lonely standalone booth, especially
after dark.
Scan before you approach. If someone is loitering or a
parked car is watching the machine, walk on.
Cover the keypad, take your cash and card quickly, and 
never count money in the open — sort it later, somewhere
safe.
Vary which ATMs you use and at what times; don't make
the same machine at the same hour part of your fixed
routine.
Be aware of daily withdrawal limits as a safety feature, not
just an inconvenience: they cap how much an express-
kidnap gang can extract.
In your own car: traffic, go-slow & junctions
Our go-slows and junctions are where snatch-and-grab and
"follow-home" attacks happen most, simply because that's
where you sit stationary and boxed in.
Doors locked, windows up whenever you're stopped or
crawling — the standard advice in every official Nigeria
travel advisory.
Leave a car's length of space ahead of you in traffic so you
can always pull out or around; never let yourself be
sandwiched bumper-to-bumper.

C H A P T E R  3
Home & Estate Security
Turning your home into a hard target
Your home should be the one place you can fully relax — the
one place Module 1 said it's safe to drop into "Gear 1." That is
only true if the layers around you are doing the watching for
you. This module shows how to build those layers so that a
criminal studying your street quietly decides your house is
too much trouble, and moves on to an easier one.
One principle runs through everything here: no single
barrier stops a determined intruder — but layers do. Your
gate, your lights, your locks, your camera, and your
household's habits are not each meant to be unbeatable.
They are meant to cost an intruder time and noise — and
time and noise are exactly what a burglar or invasion crew
cannot afford. They want to be in and out, unseen and
unheard. Every layer that slows them or raises the chance of
being noticed makes your home a worse deal than the next
one.
And for most families, this is the threat you are most likely to
face. Property offences — theft, burglary, and fraud — make
up roughly half of all reported crime in Nigeria, and
international safety advisories list home invasion and
burglary among the country's most common crimes, with the
risk climbing after dark. Even prominent, well-resourced
Nigerians have been killed in residential robberies, with
29

===== PAGE 30 =====
armed-robbery cases still making national headlines into
2026 — a sober reminder that this is not only a "rough area"
problem.
🏠
 The home-security mindset: think like the person
casing your house. Stand at your own gate after dark
and ask, "If I wanted in, where is the weak point — the
dark corner, the climbable wall, the napping gateman,
the window hidden behind the generator?" Every
weakness you find is one you can fix before someone
else finds it for you.
A quiet evening, two endings
It's 9 p.m., NEPA has taken the light, and the street is dark
except for the hum of generators. Two men have watched
one particular gate for a few evenings. They've clocked that
the security light never comes on, that the gateman naps
around this time, and that an overgrown tree shades the
back wall.
Now picture the same house built in layers: a motion light
snaps on as they approach, a clearly visible camera with
battery backup watches the gate, the wall is clear and
topped, and a dog stirs inside. Suddenly nothing about that
house is worth the risk when an easier one sits two streets
away. That is the whole game — you win not by fighting, but
by being the house they decide to skip.

C H A P T E R  4
Child & School Safety
Protecting our children at the gate and beyond
There is no fear like the fear of something happening to your
child, and no Nigerian parent needs to be told the threat is
real. The wave of school abductions that returned at the end
of 2025 made that painfully clear: in a single month, gunmen
took 25 schoolgirls from a government school in Kebbi
State — killing the vice-principal — and 315 people, 303
students and 12 teachers, from St Mary's Catholic School in
Papiri, Niger State, one of the worst mass abductions in our
history. All of the Papiri captives were eventually freed by
Christmas — but the danger did not end with them.
If anything, it has hardened in 2026. In January the UN's
human-rights office warned of a fresh surge in child
abductions, a decade on from Chibok, and civic groups now
say more than 1,000 Nigerians have been kidnapped since
the start of the year. The raids have kept coming — and have
spread well beyond the usual northern flashpoints: an
orphanage school attacked in late April (its pupils freed in
early May), more than 50 children, some as young as two,
seized from three schools in Mussa, Borno State, and 39
students and seven teachers taken from schools in Oyo
State in the South-West, a region once assumed to be safer.
Set against the more than 1,500 schoolchildren abducted
since the 2014 Chibok kidnapping and the roughly 19 million

Nigerian children already out of school, the picture is
sobering — which is exactly why a calm, practised family plan
matters now more than ever.
This module is not here to deepen that fear. It is here to turn
it into a plan. And the first thing a clear plan does is separate
two very different threats, because they call for very
different responses.
🧭
 Two different threats, two different plans.
1. Mass school abductions — the headline attacks,
where armed groups storm mostly rural boarding
schools in the North-West and North-Central and take
dozens or hundreds at once for ransom. If your child
boards in a high-risk area, this is the threat to plan
around, and most of what helps is about the school's
security and your choices about where and how they
board.
2. Everyday child-targeting — the quieter, far more
common danger most city families actually face: a child
lured or snatched at the school gate, on the walk home,
or at the market, often by someone using a trick, and
sometimes by someone the family already knows. This
is the threat your daily habits, your code word, and
what you teach your child can defeat directly.
Most of this module is about threat 2, because it is the one
within your control every single day — but we cover both.

C H A P T E R  5
Vehicle & Travel Safety
The car, the checkpoint, and the open road
For many of us the car feels like the safest place we own — a
locked, private box that takes us door to door. The truth is
more complicated. Your vehicle protects you in some
moments and exposes you in others, and the difference is
almost always where and when you are driving, and how well
you planned the trip.
There are really two very different journeys to think about.
The first is the daily city drive — the school run, the office,
the market, the go-slow home — where the main risks are
snatch-and-grab at junctions, "follow-home" robbery, and
the gate-arrival moment (all covered in Modules 2 and 3). The
second is the interstate journey — the long road to the
village, another city, or the airport — where the picture
changes completely, and the risk becomes armed robbery
and kidnapping on lonely stretches of highway.
That second risk is real and current. Our highways, especially
notorious corridors like the Abuja–Kaduna expressway, have
seen armed gangs stop vehicles to rob and abduct travellers;
as recently as January 2026 the army rescued eleven kidnap
victims in a late-night operation on that very road. Official
travel advisories through 2026 list carjacking, roadside
banditry, and kidnapping among the most common violent
crimes nationwide, and several states are flagged "do not
49

===== PAGE 50 =====
travel" outright. The steady advice across all of them is the
same: plan hard, travel by day, and never resist a robbery.
And there is a quieter danger we tend to forget while
worrying about bandits: the road itself. Crashes — from
reckless overtaking, potholes, bad tyres, and night driving —
injure and kill far more Nigerian families in an ordinary year
than armed gangs do. A complete travel plan has to treat
both.
🧭
 The travel mindset: a safe journey is mostly won before
you turn the key. The decisions that protect you — when
to travel, which road, who knows your plan, is the car
ready — are all made at home, calmly, the day before.
Once you are on a lonely highway, your options are
already mostly set. Plan the trip, and the trip almost
plans itself.
A journey, two endings
A family needs to travel to a town three hours away. In the
first version, they leave "whenever we're ready" — which
turns out to be 3 p.m. — in a car low on fuel and running on a
tyre that should have been changed last month. No one
outside the car knows the route. Darkness catches them on a
lonely stretch, the fuel runs low, and they're forced to crawl
along looking for a station, exactly where they are most
exposed.
Now the same trip, planned. They decide the night before to
leave at first light, fill the tank and check the tyres in the

travel in daylight, keep moving, and are sitting down to lunch
at their destination before the dangerous hours begin. Same

decided entirely by choices made at home.
Before you travel — the planning that does most
of the work
Ask whether the trip is necessary, and when. For long
trips through higher-risk areas, weigh whether a flight, a
different date, or a safer route is worth it. If you must go, 
timing is everything.
Travel by day, always. Leave early so you arrive well before
dark. The most dangerous hours on any highway are dusk
to dawn — for both bandits and crashes. Avoid night road
travel outside city centres entirely.
Get current road intelligence. Roads change week to
week. Ask people who ply the route regularly, or contacts
in the area, which stretches are safe right now and which
to avoid. A road that was fine last year may be a black spot
today.
Prepare the vehicle the day before. Service it, fill the tank
(never set off low — a fuel stop in the wrong place is a
vulnerable moment), and check tyres, spare, and tools. A
breakdown on a lonely road is one of the most dangerous
traps there is.

travelling, and your expected arrival time with at least

A few one-time investments make your vehicle a harder

C H A P T E R  6
If the Worst Happens
Kidnap, ransom & getting your people home
This is the chapter we all pray never to need. Read it once,
calmly, while your mind is clear — because if that day ever
comes, no one in the house will be thinking straight, and you
will want to have already decided what to do. Everything in
Modules 1 to 5 exists so that you never reach this page. But
preparation is not pessimism. A family that has quietly agreed
who does what, and who to call, is a family that can act in the
first frightening minutes instead of freezing.
Let us be honest about where we stand, without losing our
heads over it. Kidnapping in Nigeria has hardened into an
organised, profit-driven business. The most detailed
independent tracking we have, from SBM Intelligence,
recorded at least 4,722 people abducted in 997 incidents in
the year to June 2025, with at least 762 killed; kidnappers
demanded roughly ₦48 billion and collected about ₦2.57
billion. The year before that, other trackers counted more
than 7,500 abductions and over 1,000 deaths. And here is the
hard truth every family must sit with before it ever
negotiates: paying is not a guarantee. In one widely reported
case in mid-2025, captors killed dozens of hostages even
after a ransom had been paid. None of this is meant to
frighten you — it is meant to make sure that if you ever act,
you act with clear eyes.

⚠️
 This module is calm, general survival guidance — not
legal or professional negotiation advice. Two things you
must know up front. First, under a 2022 law, paying a
ransom is technically a crime in Nigeria — a minimum
15-year term, with the death penalty for kidnappers
where a victim dies — though it is rarely enforced and
most affected families still pay. Second, serious cases
are beyond any guide: the moment you can, bring in the
police and, where possible, professionals who do this
for a living. Use what follows to survive the first hours
and to think clearly, not as a substitute for expert help.
Know which situation you are in
Not every "kidnap" is the same, and the right response
depends entirely on which one you are facing:
Express kidnapping — held for a few hours, driven
between ATMs and made to withdraw and transfer money;
it usually ends when the money runs out. (Covered in
Module 2.) Comply — your life over your account.
Targeted kidnap-for-ransom — you or a relative is
marked, taken, and held for days or weeks while a ransom
is negotiated. This is where the rest of this module
focuses.
Mass, highway, or school abduction — armed groups take
many people at once, usually on rural roads or from
schools. Prevention lives in Modules 4 and 5; the survival
advice below still applies.

Your single goal in captivity is survival. Not bravery, not

worth far more to their captors alive than dead, and that

cooperative. Follow their rules. Don't argue, don't
challenge, don't stare them down, don't make threats. The
more human and unremarkable you are, the safer you stay.

C H A P T E R  7
Digital & Financial Safety
Protecting your money, identity & family online
Up to now this playbook has guarded your body, your home,
and your movements. This module guards something
criminals are coming after just as hard — and far more
quietly: your money, your identity, and your family's
presence online. You will never see this attacker's face.
There is no okada at your gate, no checkpoint, no masked
man. There is only a phone call that sounds official, a link
that looks real, an investment that sounds too good, or a
stranger who slid into your child's DMs. And the damage can
empty in minutes what took years to build.
Here is the encouraging part, and it runs through this whole
guide: the same calm habits that keep you safe on the road
keep you safe online. Slow down. Verify on a separate
channel. Never act under pressure. Urgency is the
fraudster's only real weapon — and patience is yours.
The numbers tell a hopeful story wrapped around a warning.
Nigerian banks lost about ₦25.85 billion to digital-payment
fraud in 2025 — down 51% from ₦52.26 billion in 2024, with
reported cases falling to roughly 67,515, the fifth straight year
of decline, helped by BVN–NIN identity checks. That is real
progress. But the warning is just as real: Lagos alone
accounts for about 63% of fraud, and in early 2026 the
Central Bank issued public alerts about fraudulent links,

===== PAGE 69 =====
emails and messages, calling the scale of digital-payment
scams "catastrophic" and pushing banks toward stronger
authentication. The thieves haven't given up — they've gone
smarter, more personal, and increasingly powered by AI.
🧭
 The one rule that defeats almost every scam: no
genuine request ever requires you to act this second. A
real bank, a real official, a real relative in trouble will all
survive you hanging up and calling back on a number

talking to the scam.
A quiet afternoon, two endings
A trader gets a call. The voice is calm, professional, and

To reverse it, read me the code we just sent — quickly, before

learns there was never any fraud team, never any problem —

Threat 1 — The fake bank call, OTP & USSD trap
This is the most common way ordinary Nigerians lose money,
and it almost always relies on you handing over the keys. The
fraudster, posing as your bank, NIBSS, a switching company,
or even "EFCC, " creates panic — a blocked account, a
suspicious transfer, an expiring BVN — and rushes you into
revealing what they cannot get on their own.
Never share an OTP, PIN, CVV, or full card number —
with anyone, ever. No real bank or staff member will ever
ask for these. An OTP is the last lock on your money;
reading it aloud is opening your own safe for a thief.
Be deeply suspicious of any USSD code a "helper" tells
you to dial. Some strings forward your calls (enabling SIM-
related fraud) or authorise transfers. If you didn't initiate
it, don't dial it.
Hang up and call back on a number you already have —
the one on your card, your bank's app, or its official
website. Never the number that called you, and never a
number they give you.
Treat unexpected "you've been credited" alerts with
suspicion. A common market scam is a fake credit alert (a
spoofed SMS or a doctored screenshot) used to collect
goods or "refund" an overpayment. Confirm money has
truly landed in your own banking app before you release
anything.

Threat 2 — SIM-swap: when they steal your
phone number
This is the quiet one that empties accounts overnight, and
regulators say it is on the rise. A fraudster gathers enough of
your details (often from your own oversharing) to convince
your network to move your number onto their SIM. Suddenly
your line goes dead — and every OTP, every alert, every
"forgot password" code now goes to them.
Your first red flag is sudden loss of service — no calls, no
texts, "SIM not registered" — for no reason, especially after
odd calls or messages. Treat an unexplained dead line as
an emergency: contact your network and bank at once
from another phone.
Set a SIM/network PIN and ask your provider what extra
port-out or SIM-swap protection they offer.
Move beyond SMS codes where you can. Use an 
authenticator app or in-app approval for banking and
email rather than SMS OTPs, so a stolen number alone
can't unlock your accounts.
Lock down the email tied to your bank with its own
strong password and app-based two-factor — it's the
master key to everything else.

Threat 7 — Your digital footprint: don't do the
criminal's homework
Everything in Module 1 about being "marked" now happens
first on a screen. Your posts are free intelligence — for
kidnappers pricing a ransom, burglars timing your absence,
and scammers building rapport.
Don't broadcast wealth, big purchases, or cash. The new

Protecting our children online — the predator
you can't see
The digital threat to children is real enough that Nigeria
acted on it: the National Assembly passed a Child Online
Protection Bill at the end of 2025, and the NCC, NAPTIP and
UNICEF all run active child-online-safety programmes.
Predators no longer need the school gate of Module 4 — they
reach in through games, social apps, and DMs, often posing
as another child, grooming patiently, then moving to threats
and sextortion.
Keep devices in shared spaces, especially for younger
children, and know which apps and games they use and
who they talk to there.
Set the golden rule: the online code from Module 4
applies on screens too — no real friend asks you to keep
secrets from your parents, send photos, or meet in person.
Watch for grooming and sextortion signs: a child who is
suddenly secretive about their phone, anxious after going
online, or in contact with someone older they "met" in a
game. Sextortion — tricking a child into a compromising
photo, then blackmailing them — is rising and thrives on a
child's fear of being blamed.
Keep the door open. As in Module 4, a child who knows
they can tell you anything without being scolded is your
strongest protection. Make clear they will never be in
trouble for reporting something that scared them.
Use parental controls and privacy settings, but treat
them as a backup to conversation, not a substitute for it.

C H A P T E R  8
Your Family Emergency
Plan
One plan your whole household has practised
Everything in this playbook so far has been a layer of
protection. This final module is the thread that ties all those
layers into one thing your whole family can pick up and use
on the worst day of your life. Because here is the quiet truth
behind every emergency: in a real crisis, nobody rises to the
occasion — you fall back to what you have already
practised. Fear floods the mind, the hands shake, and clear
thinking is the first thing to go. A family that has talked
through its plan, agreed who does what, and drilled it twice
will act in those first frightening minutes while everyone else
is still frozen.
This module is short on new theory and long on doing. By the
end of it you should have a single, written Family Emergency
Plan — code words, contacts, meeting points, a go-bag, and a
document vault — that every member of your household
knows. It draws together the worksheets you filled in across
Modules 1 to 7 and turns them into one calm, shared system.

🧭
 The whole point of a plan: an emergency is the worst
possible time to be making decisions for the first time.
Everything you decide now — calmly, over a quiet
evening — is one less thing to work out while your
heart is pounding. The plan is not paperwork. It is pre-
made calm.
Two families, one emergency
It is 8 p.m. The lights are out, and there is a sudden, hard
banging at the gate — voices, urgency, something wrong. In
the first home, panic takes over. The children scatter, no one
knows where to go, the phones are charging in three
different rooms, nobody can remember the estate security
number, and precious minutes are lost in pure confusion.
Now the same night in a family with a plan. One word is
spoken — their agreed "act now" code word — and everyone
moves the way they have practised: the children go straight
to the safe room, the steadiest adult grabs the charged
phone that lives in a known spot and calls for help, the gate is
not opened. Nothing about the danger is smaller. But this
family is acting while the other is still reeling. That difference
— between a plan on paper and a panic in the dark — is what
this module is here to build.

Part 1 — The contact tree and the point-person
When something goes wrong, confusion multiplies if
everyone is calling everyone. A simple contact tree fixes who
reaches whom.
Name one calm point-person. As in Module 6, this is the
steadiest adult — often not the most emotionally involved
one — who takes the lead, makes the key calls, and keeps
the family coordinated. Name a backup too, in case the
first is the one in trouble.
Draw the tree. Decide, on paper, who calls the police, who
calls the rest of the family, who calls the trusted neighbour
or estate security. When each person has one clear job,
nothing important gets dropped.
Agree a single "check-in" contact outside your home — a
relative in another area — who everyone can call to report
"I'm safe" if the family gets separated. In many
emergencies it is easier to reach someone far away than
someone across town.

Part 4 — Meeting points and "what do we do if…"
Families get separated in emergencies — a fire, an attack, a

A Final Word
We began this playbook with an honest look at a hard reality,
so let us end with something just as true: you were never
powerless, and you are far less so now.
Look back at how far we have come together. You can read a
street and a room the way a professional does. You know
how criminals choose, watch, and move — and how to quietly
become the target they decide to skip. You have hardened
your home in layers, made your daily movements less
predictable, thought through the school gate and the open
road, prepared your mind for the worst day, locked down
your money and your family's life online, and pulled it all into
one plan your household can actually use. That is not a small
thing. That is a family transformed.
If this playbook has a single heartbeat, it is this: safety is not
fear, and it is not paranoia — it is calm, it is preparation,
and above all it is love made practical. Everything in these
pages exists not to make you smaller and more afraid, but to
make you freer: free to travel, to do business, to send your
children to school, to build the life you want, carrying a quiet
confidence instead of a low, constant dread. The aware
family is not the tense one. It is the one that has stopped
worrying, because it has prepared.

the point. Pick one thing this week — agree your code words,
